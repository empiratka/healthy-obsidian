## The Influence of Meal Frequency and Timing on Health in Humans: The Role of Fasting

type: [[study_design_systematic_review|systematic review]]
## Findings
- [[behavior_meal_frequency|Meal Frequency]]
	- [[behavior_meal_frequency_low|1–2 times/d]] is worse than [[behavior_meal_frequency_high|>3 times/d]] and [[behavior_meal_frequency_medium|3 times/d]] 
		- ([[study_design_cross-sectional|cross-sectional]]) Cholesterol, Body Weight and Diabetes
			- Worsening of [[metric_blood_lipids|blood lipids]] associated with a [[behavior_meal_frequency_low|“gorging”]] (a reduced meal frequency, one or two meals daily) diet compared to [[behavior_meal_frequency_high|“nibbling”]] (the consumption of frequent smaller meals or snacks).
				- Relevant during the transition to an “gorging” diet within the first 1–2 weeks.[^8]
				- Relevant for [[condition_obesity|overweight]], [[condition_hypercholesterolaemia|hypercholesterolaemia]], [[condition_impaired_glucose_tolerance|diminished glucose tolerance]] and "normal".[^9]
			- A [[behavior_meal_frequency_low|reduced meal frequency]] is associated with an increased risk of [[condition_cardiovascular_disease|CVDs]].[^10]
			- A lower age-adjusted [[metric_blood_lipids_total_cholesterol|TC]] and [[metric_blood_lipids_ldl_cholesterol|LDL]] cholesterol in subjects who reported eating [[behavior_meal_frequency_high|four or more meals daily]], compared to those reporting [[behavior_meal_frequency_low|one or two]].[^11]
				- The association was also confirmed after adjustment for [[alcohol]], [[smoking]], [[systolic blood pressure]], anthropometric measurements as [[metric_anthropometric_ratio_waist_to_hip|WHR]] and [[metric_anthropometric_body_mass_index|BMI]], and macronutrient intake
			- A lower concentration of [[metric_blood_lipids_total_cholesterol|TC]] and [[metric_blood_lipids_ldl_cholesterol|LDL]] cholesterol in subjects reporting a [[behavior_meal_frequency_high|higher]] (≥6 times/day) meal frequency compared to those who ate [[behavior_meal_frequency_low|1 or 2 times a day]], even when adjusted for [[age]], [[metric_anthropometric_body_mass_index|BMI]], [[physical activity]], [[smoking]], [[total energy intake]], and [[macronutrient distribution]][^13]
			- A [[behavior_meal_frequency_high|frequency higher than four times per day]] leads to a lower risk of [[condition_obesity|obesity]] compared to a [[behavior_meal_frequency_low|frequency lower than three times per day]], even after adjustment for [[age]], [[sex]], [[physical activity]], and [[total energy intake]][^14]
			- Eating [[behavior_meal_frequency_low|three or fewer meals per d]] was also associated with increased likelihood of general and central [[condition_obesity|obesity]] in [[context_sex_male|men]].[^15]
				- moreover, after adjustment for [[diet]] and [[lifestyle]], frequent eaters had lower [[metric_anthropometric_waist|waist circumference]].
				- However, results did not reach statistical significance among [[context_sex_female|women]].
			- An increased risk of [[condition_diabetes_type2|T2DM]] in men who ate [[behavior_meal_frequency_low|1–2 times a day]] compared to those who ate [[behavior_meal_frequency_medium|three meals a day]] (relative risk RR 1.26) after adjustments for [[age]], [[metric_anthropometric_body_mass_index|BMI]], and other relevant factors[^16]
		- Weight Control
			- #todo [^26]
			- #todo [^27]
			- The results showed that eating one or two meals daily was associated with a relatively lower BMI compared with three meals daily. Interestingly, they found a positive relationship between the number of meals and snacks (more than three daily) and increases in BMI.[^28]
			- Both older studies [29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6520689/#B29-nutrients-11-00719),[30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6520689/#B30-nutrients-11-00719) and more recent research [31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6520689/#B31-nutrients-11-00719) seem to suggest that a higher meal frequency can reduce weight gain risk
			- however, recent large prospective studies seem to support that frequent snacking increases the risk of weight gain [32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6520689/#B32-nutrients-11-00719),[33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6520689/#B33-nutrients-11-00719) and type 2 diabetes [^16][^17]
	- In a 1989 paper, authors compared a very high frequency of meals (17) to a lower frequency (3) and found an improvement of total and LDL cholesterol with the higher frequency; however, this particular approach is clearly atypical in ordinary life[^12]
	- no correlation between increased meal frequency and type 2 diabetes risk in women after six years follow up (3 times a day: RR 1.09, ≥6 times a day: RR 0.99)[^17]
	- men eating 1–2 meals per day had an RR for CHD of 1.10, men eating 4–5 meals per day had an RR of 1.05, and men eating ≥6 times had an RR 1.26, as compared to who ate three times a day after adjustment for total energy intake, diet composition, and other risk factors[^18]
